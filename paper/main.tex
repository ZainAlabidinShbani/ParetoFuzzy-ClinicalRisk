\begin{abstract}
Clinical risk scores must discriminate, be calibrated and be interpretable, yet
genetic-fuzzy models for health risk are optimised for accuracy alone and rarely
compared with tuned gradient-boosted trees. We propose a zero-order Takagi--Sugeno
additive fuzzy risk score whose linguistic partitions and rules are evolved by NSGA-II
for three objectives: $1-$AUROC, the Brier score (a strictly proper scoring rule) and
the total number of rule literals. Rule weights are fitted by penalised logistic
regression with an inner-validated penalty. In a preregistered, leakage-free study
(\resNSeeds{}$\times$5-fold cross-validation, \resNFits{} model fits, code hashes locked
before four held-out datasets were unlocked) comparing eleven models on six public
clinical datasets, the proposed score used on average \resMOLiteralsAll{} literals, against
\resHybridLiteralsAll{} for a single-objective genetic-fuzzy model. Its mean AUROC was
\resMOAurocAll{} and its mean Brier score \resMOBrierAll{} (CatBoost: \resMeanAurocAllCatBoost{}
and \resMeanBrierAllCatBoost{}). Preregistered hypotheses:
calibration advantage over accuracy-driven genetic-fuzzy models (H1) \resHOne{};
AUROC within 0.02 of the best gradient-boosted tree on \resHTwoN{}/6 datasets (H2)
\resHTwo{}; calibration-slope advantage (H3) \resHThree{}; at most half the literals of a
single-objective genetic-fuzzy model without material AUROC loss (H4) \resHFour{}.
Optimising a proper scoring rule alongside complexity yields rule-based risk scores of
a few rules that are much better calibrated than accuracy-driven genetic-fuzzy models.
They approach, but do not match, tuned gradient-boosted trees; the remaining gap is
largest on near-separable data.
\end{abstract}
